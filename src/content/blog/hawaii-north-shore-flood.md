On March 20, 2026, a Kona Low weather system stalled over Oahu and dropped heavy rain across the island's North Shore. Within hours, streets turned into rivers. Floodwater surrounded homes, cut off roads, and forced residents onto rooftops. A warning at Wahiawa Dam triggered evacuation orders for approximately 5,000 people.

The dam held. But the flooding was already underway, and the damage was already being done.

## The Storm

Kona Lows are not hurricanes. They are upper-level low-pressure systems that form west of Hawaii and drift over the islands, pulling moisture from the tropics. They move slowly. When they stall, the rain does not stop.

This one stalled over Oahu. Rainfall rates exceeded what the island's drainage infrastructure could handle. Water pooled in low-lying neighborhoods along the North Shore, rising fast enough that some residents had no time to drive out. By midday, the flooding had turned residential streets into channels carrying debris, mud, and standing water several feet deep.

## The Dam Warning

Wahiawa Dam sits upstream of communities on Oahu's central plain. As rainfall continued, water levels in the reservoir rose. Authorities issued a warning for the dam, not because it had failed, but because the rate of inflow raised concerns about its capacity.

The evacuation order covered approximately 5,000 people in areas downstream of the dam. For residents already dealing with flooded roads and rising water, the dam warning added urgency to an already dangerous situation. Some evacuated on foot. Others could not leave at all.

Engineers monitored the dam throughout the event. It remained structurally intact. The warning was lifted after water levels stabilized, but the evacuation order stood until officials confirmed conditions were safe.

## Rooftop Rescues

The U.S. Coast Guard deployed crews from Air Station Barbers Point. Helicopters flew over flooded neighborhoods on the North Shore, identifying residents stranded on rooftops and upper floors. The rescue footage, captured by Coast Guard and military cameras, shows what urban flood rescue looks like in a place where the terrain funnels water toward the coast.

Navy personnel joined the response. The 25th Combat Aviation Brigade provided helicopter support. The Hawaii National Guard's 117th Mobile Public Affairs Detachment documented the operations. Rescue swimmers entered floodwater to reach residents who could not be hoisted directly.

The rescues were methodical. Each person was assessed, secured, and lifted. In some cases, pets were rescued alongside their owners. The footage shows the repetitive precision of the work: hover, lower the swimmer, secure the survivor, hoist, move to the next roof.

## Assessment and Cleanup

The immediate rescue phase transitioned into assessment. On March 21, teams began surveying the damage. Roads were impassable in several areas. Debris covered yards and driveways. Mud coated the interiors of flooded homes.

Operations continued on March 24 and March 26 as crews worked to restore access, clear debris, and assess structural damage to homes and infrastructure. FEMA's Daily Operations Brief on March 21 included the Hawaii flooding on page 3, noting the scope of the response.

The University of Hawaii's Mesonet recorded rainfall data that confirmed the intensity of the event. The numbers matched what residents experienced: rain that came too fast, lasted too long, and overwhelmed a landscape that channels water downhill toward communities built in its path.

## A Familiar Pattern

Hawaii's geography makes it uniquely vulnerable to flash flooding. Steep mountains, narrow valleys, and short distances between ridgeline and coast mean that heavy rain reaches populated areas quickly. Drainage systems designed for normal rainfall cannot handle the volume that a stalled Kona Low delivers.

The North Shore flooding followed a pattern that has repeated across Hawaii's history. Heavy rain, rapid runoff, flooded neighborhoods, and rescue operations that depend on military and Coast Guard assets because civilian resources are quickly overwhelmed.

The dam held. The rescues succeeded. The cleanup continued for days. But the footage from March 20 shows how quickly water can turn a neighborhood into a rescue zone, and how narrow the margin is between a warning and a disaster.
